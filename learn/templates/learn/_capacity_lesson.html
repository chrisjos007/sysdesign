Turn a million users into a workload

A registered-user count cannot size a service. Start with what active learners do, then change one assumption and follow its effect.

Unscored browser exercise. Reloading resets your choices; the quiz drives XP and review.

Separate three kinds of requirement

Before calculating, decide which statement describes behavior, which must always hold, and which needs a measurement window.

  • A learner opens 20 lessons per day.
  • A private lesson is never returned to an unauthorized learner.
  • Over a rolling 30 days, 99% of successful lesson reads finish within 300 ms at the API boundary.
Compare your reasoning

The first is a workload assumption to validate. The second is an invariant: faster responses cannot excuse breaking it. The third is a latency objective with a population, boundary, and window. Track failed requests separately so excluding them from this latency measure cannot hide outages.

Follow the units

Assume 100,000 daily active learners, 20 reads and five events per learner per day. Each response carries 10 KB; each event uses 500 bytes. All units are decimal: 1 KB = 1,000 bytes and 1 GB = 1,000,000,000 bytes.

2,000,000 reads/day ÷ 86,400 s/day = 23.15 reads/s average.

23.15 × 20 ≈ 462.96 reads/s at peak.

20 GB/day of response payload; 37.04 Mbit/s at peak.

250 MB/day × 30 days = 7.5 GB of raw events.

Why do these numbers change differently?

Daily reads = active learners × 20. Peak rate = daily reads ÷ 86,400 × peak factor. Payload bytes = reads × 10,000; multiply peak bytes/s by eight for bits/s. Event bytes/day = active learners × 5 × 500. Retention multiplies storage, not daily writes. Increasing the peak factor redistributes the same daily traffic into a sharper burst.

Payload excludes headers and compression. Raw storage excludes indexes, replicas, backups, and deletion lag. Budget those separately. A cache may spare origin reads while users still receive the response bytes.

Try the curriculum challenge

Keep 100,000 active learners; predict the result with 40× peak and 365-day retention before selecting them. Expect about 926 reads/s and 91.25 GB of raw events. With the invented 200 reads/s observation, five servers must remain available; provisioning six leaves five after one fails. This alone does not prove the latency objective is met.
