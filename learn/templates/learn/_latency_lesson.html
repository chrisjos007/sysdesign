Watch the waiting work

Throughput counts completions per second. Latency is one request’s elapsed time: network travel, queue waiting, service time, and response transfer. Service time covers only the work at a resource.

Build a backlog. Then clear it.

120 jobs arrive each second, but the workers can complete only 100. Predict the backlog after five minutes. Then reduce arrivals to 80 jobs/s and predict how long it takes to drain.

Rate changes apply to the next interval. They do not change the existing backlog.

0 minutes elapsed. 0 jobs waiting. No interval completed yet.

Total arrivals: 0 jobs. Total completions: 0 jobs.

The queue is empty. At these rates, it will grow by 20 jobs/s.

Check the five-minute prediction

(120 − 100) × 300 = 6,000 jobs waiting. With arrivals reduced to 80/s, only 20 jobs/s of capacity is available to clear that backlog: 6,000 ÷ 20 = 300 seconds, another five minutes. At 100 arrivals/s, the existing backlog never shrinks.

A fluid estimate: smooth arrivals, equal-cost jobs, fixed capacity per interval, unlimited waiting space, no failures, retries, or drops. It estimates waiting work, not individual latency percentiles. More queue space cannot fix sustained overload.

Same throughput. More work in flight.

For a stable system, Little’s Law is L = λ × W. Use average completed throughput and mean response time measured across the same boundary, including waiting and service.

200 requests/s × 0.15 s = 30 requests in flight on average.

Try 600 ms at 200 requests/s: average in-flight work rises from 30 to 120 requests. Those requests can occupy memory and sockets while waiting, even when CPU usage is low.

This is a separate steady-state example, not a measurement of the growing queue above. Little’s Law does not predict p99, prove stability, or determine how many servers you need.

Nearest-rank convention: sort 1,000 samples and select rank ceil(percentile × 1,000). Other conventions can differ. These samples contain no errors or drops; in production, report offered traffic, completions, errors, and latency together.

Explain the bottleneck

A service completes 50 requests/s with a mean response time of 200 ms. Calculate average in-flight work. Then explain why low CPU does not rule out a queueing problem.

50 × 0.2 = 10 requests on average, provided the system is stable and the measurement boundaries match. Requests can wait for locks, connections, or downstream responses without using much CPU. Measure where time is spent before adding workers.
